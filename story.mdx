<Chapter id="c1" eyebrow="Honestly" title="The part I usually leave out">

The fear hit hardest in an early session on agents. I had a feeling I last had in 2013, when my on-premises skills suddenly looked old. Only this time it was moving faster. "I did this once," I remember thinking. "Can I do it again?"

<p className="lead">I was afraid of becoming irrelevant. I doubted myself. I still struggle a bit with imposter syndrome, even after everything you're about to read.</p>

If you work in infrastructure and any of that sounds familiar, keep reading. I'm not telling this story because I have it all figured out. I'm telling it for you, not about me, because I've been here before. So have you.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="c2" eyebrow="2013 · Change" title="It started in 2013">

In February 2013, I joined Microsoft as a Partner Technology Strategist. My job was to help partners become cloud-first, and that meant I had to make the change myself. I built on my on-premises infrastructure experience and grew into a cloud-first infrastructure architect. I had to practice the change I was asking partners to make.

I see a similar opportunity today. AI tools can help infrastructure architects and engineers extend their work beyond traditional infrastructure, while building on the experience they already have. If I can do it, you can.

<Photo src="images/jonathan-vella-2016.jpg" alt="Jonathan Vella in 2016, wearing a dark T-shirt that reads Keep calm, put it on Azure" caption="Me in 2016. The T-shirt says it all." width="401" height="501" />

<ForYou label="For you">You have changed before. Your experience is the foundation, not the obstacle.</ForYou>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="c3" eyebrow="Before AI" title="Where I was before AI">

This is what a single piece of architecture work can involve.

<Scope label="What a piece of architecture work can involve">

1. Business outcomes
2. Non-functional requirements
3. Compliance and regulation
4. Budget and timelines
5. Initial architecture
6. Cost estimates
7. Well-Architected review
8. Azure Policy alignment
9. Infrastructure as code
10. Deployment
11. Adjustments
12. Documentation

</Scope>

Non-functional requirements are the reliability, security and performance a system must meet.

<p className="pull">Before AI, that could take me days or weeks.</p>

I work across three continents, so time and scale are major constraints in my engagements. And here's an admission. Before AI, I did not do much infrastructure-as-code work. I had limited time, and I did not feel especially strong in IaC.

Now I write and share reusable infrastructure code and snippets with my audiences. Working with AI helped me turn a weak spot into part of my everyday work, and deliver faster.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="c4" eyebrow="May 2025 · Use" title="The first change">

In May 2025, I discovered that agents in GitHub could help me create architecture diagrams, cost estimates and Well-Architected reviews much faster. By mid-2025, connecting agents to live cloud context and reusable skills became part of my work.

The first useful result came from an e-shop project I worked on alone. GitHub Copilot helped me create the infrastructure as code, deploy it, and produce Python-based architecture and data-flow diagrams. Before deployment, I checked that the infrastructure as code aligned with my policy constraints.

<Split label="Rough split of the implementation output on the e-shop project" aiLabel="Copilot ~60%" meLabel="Me ~40%" ai={60} me={40}>My rough estimate of the implementation output. I did not measure it. [How to read my numbers](#numbers)</Split>

It was a small moment. I had done very little infrastructure as code before, and now I had working code that I could read, check and stand behind. That was when AI started to feel less like a threat to my work and more like a way to extend it.

<ForYou label="For you">Start with one task you repeat, not your whole job.</ForYou>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="c5" eyebrow="Guardrail" title="Verify, you must">

In May 2025, agentic AI was still new in my work, and I saw it build one hallucination on top of another. In my experience, it is more reliable now. Those early months also taught me not to overload prompts. Too much information can get you nowhere with AI. Less can be more.

Around October 2025, while I was building my [Microsoft Sovereign Cloud Brain Trek](https://jonathan-vella.github.io/microsoft-sovereign-cloud-brain-trek/), an AI agent gave me a confident answer about Microsoft's digital commitments. When I challenged it, the agent produced a fake URL. So I checked the official announcement.

<Versus>
<Claim label="What the AI told me" headline="Six digital commitments, announced in December 2024">Backed by a link that turned out to be fake.</Claim>
<Fact label="What the official announcement says" headline="Five digital commitments to Europe, announced on 30 April 2025">[Microsoft announces new European digital commitments](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2025/04/30/european-digital-commitments/)</Fact>
</Versus>

A confident answer is not the same as a correct one. That episode reminded me to verify AI-generated claims and links, every time.

<ForYou label="For you">Verify, you must. Every claim, every link.</ForYou>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="c6" eyebrow="Stretch" title="What AI let me take on">

One partner engagement showed me how far AI could stretch my work. I owned the initial design for a multi-region AKS/PostgreSQL platform. AI helped me work through the CloudNativePG operator and PostgreSQL practices, build the load-testing setup and create a Grafana dashboard.

It was not a straight line. We tested the documented non-functional requirements, I reviewed the reports and supporting evidence, and we had to iterate and troubleshoot rather than accept the first output. When an early suggestion to use Azure Container Instances for load testing caused too much friction, I switched to a separate AKS cluster with enough capacity to generate the load.

<p className="joke"><span className="label">Dad joke</span>Azure Container Instances could not contain my requirements. Sorry.</p>

<Stats>
<Stat figure="17,000">transactions per second for five minutes, with zero data loss</Stat>
<Stat figure="~8 s">failover during an unexpected database-node outage</Stat>
<Stat figure="~50 h">of work in under a week, addressing PCI and GDPR requirements</Stat>
</Stats>

<p className="note-link">From my own tests. <a href="#numbers">How to read my numbers</a></p>

AI did not make my infrastructure experience less important. It gave me room to stretch into application and database modernisation—work I would previously have handed off or avoided. To see how far I could push that, I built a [hands-on modernisation lab kit](https://factory.apexops.pro/) end to end with AI, apart from the Contoso University sample application. It brings together cloud foundations, application modernisation, database migration, performance testing, validation and handover.

I built it alone over a matter of weeks. My estimate is that it would typically have taken two architects about two months. Building it showed me how much broader an infrastructure architect's reach can become when curiosity replaces hesitation.

<Chapter id="c7" eyebrow="Late 2025 to 2026 · Build" title="From shortcuts to a workflow">

As the results became repeatable, I stopped treating them as personal shortcuts and started building them into [a multi-agent workflow](https://github.com/jonathan-vella/apex) that turns platform engineering requirements into Bicep or Terraform, with human approval gates along the way.

<Gates title="AI prepares, humans decide" label="Gate" caption="Human approval gates for requirements, architecture, plan and deployment.">

1. Requirements
2. Architecture
3. Plan
4. Deployment

</Gates>

It grew from a shared resource for IT pros that I built with input from colleagues.

The speed has a cost. Much of my time now goes into reading, checking and correcting what AI produces. It's a different kind of work, not less work.

<p className="joke"><span className="label">Dad joke</span>I asked AI to make my architecture highly available. It said it would be right back.</p>

<ForYou label="For you">When something works twice, turn it into a workflow.</ForYou>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="c10" eyebrow="Foundation" title="What I still own">

<Signature caption="My way of putting it. I am always in the driving seat.">ai has the intelligence, i have the wisdom.</Signature>

My infrastructure background still matters in that work. It helps me consider the platform, security, resilience and operation of the modernised service, while AI helps me take on more of the application and database work. It does not make me the only specialist a real modernisation effort needs. I work with people who bring deeper application or data expertise, and I involve them in decisions beyond my own experience.

I cannot approve that application for production on my own. I do not have all the development skills and experience needed to challenge some of the AI-generated outputs, and a passing build and successful tests do not give me that experience. For production, I team up with someone who has it, such as a senior developer, to review the code and make the development decisions I am not qualified to approve.

<Workflow label="Human, AI and specialist workflow">
<Phase role="Me" title="Discover">The business problem, why it needs solving, and how success is measured.</Phase>
<Phase role="Me" title="Develop">The technical architecture.</Phase>
<Phase role="AI-assisted" title="Use AI" kind="ai">To assist with analysis, code and iteration.</Phase>
<Phase role="Me · human check" title="Check the output" kind="check">Against requirements, Azure Policy constraints and available evidence.</Phase>
<Phase role="Specialist, when needed" title="Specialist input" kind="spec">For decisions beyond my expertise.</Phase>
<Phase role="Qualified people" title="Production approval" kind="spec">I do not approve applications for production on my own.</Phase>
</Workflow>

AI expands the work I can do. It does not remove my responsibility to recognise where my expertise ends.

I know that boundary because I've hit it. The build passed and the tests were green, and I still couldn't tell whether the application code AI had written was any good. I remember thinking, "I'm an infra person pretending to be a developer." That's imposter syndrome talking, and sometimes it's right. The answer is not to pretend. It's to bring in someone who does.

<p className="yoda">Much to learn, I still have.</p>

<ForYou label="For you">Know where your expertise ends, and bring in people who start there.</ForYou>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="skills" eyebrow="Mindset · Adapt to thrive" title="Find what AI equips you to do">

You don't adapt to AI by memorising prompts or collecting certificates. You adapt by staying curious, testing your edges, and changing how you work.

<SkillsRoadmap label="How to adapt and thrive with AI">
<SkillStage title="1. Trade fear for curiosity">
When a new capability appears, notice the hesitation—and try it on a real, low-risk task anyway. Ask "What happens if I try this?" instead of "Will this replace me?"

**In practice:** pick one task you normally avoid or repeat every week, and spend an hour exploring how an agent handles it.
</SkillStage>
<SkillStage title="2. Discover the capabilities it unlocks">
AI does not replace your foundation; it equips you to reach across boundaries that used to stop you—whether that is writing infrastructure code, testing database failover, or modernising an application in a lab.

**In practice:** use your core experience as the anchor, and let AI help you step into one adjacent area you used to hand off.
</SkillStage>
<SkillStage title="3. Shift from doing everything to directing and verifying">
Thriving with AI means changing how you spend your energy. Less time goes into typing from scratch; more goes into framing the problem, steering agents, verifying every output, and knowing when to bring in a specialist.

**In practice:** turn one successful experiment into a repeatable workflow with explicit human approval gates.
</SkillStage>
</SkillsRoadmap>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="c11" eyebrow="This week" title="Your turn">

There is no try, as Yoda said, but there is a low-risk first step.

<FirstSteps stepLabel="Step">
<FirstStep title="Ask better questions">
Use AI to help prepare the questions that uncover business requirements. As architects, we need to know which problem or problems we are solving, why they need to be solved, and how we will measure success before we move on to technical architecture.

**Try this prompt:**

```
You're helping me prepare a discovery workshop for a customer moving a workload to Azure. Draft 15 questions that uncover the business problem, why it needs solving now, how success will be measured, and any compliance, budget or timeline limits. Group them by theme.
```
</FirstStep>
<FirstStep title="Pick one repeated task"><p>Let AI draft it, then check it against something you trust, such as an existing diagram or Azure Advisor.</p></FirstStep>
<FirstStep title="Know your edge"><p>Write down where your expertise ends and who you will ask beyond it.</p></FirstStep>
</FirstSteps>

<Practice title="The whole practice on one screen" caption="AI widens the work I take on. It does not make me an expert in every specialty.">
<PracticeStages>
<PracticeStage number="1" title="Use">Start with one repeated task.</PracticeStage>
<PracticeStage number="2" title="Build">Turn repeated work into a workflow.</PracticeStage>
<PracticeStage number="3" title="Scale">Share it.</PracticeStage>
</PracticeStages>
<p className="guardrails"><strong>Guardrails at every stage.</strong> Verify every output. Bring in specialists for decisions beyond my expertise.</p>
<p className="foundation"><strong>Infrastructure expertise</strong> stays the foundation at every stage.</p>
</Practice>

So, can I do it again? Yes. Some days the fear is still there, but most days it has turned into curiosity. "Can I keep up?" became "What can I try next?"
